How to read this dashboard.

It answers one question twice a day: how worried should I be, and is it getting better or worse? Everything else is the evidence behind that answer.

Reading it in a minute

  1. Read the headline. It names the pattern the data matches best right now (today: scenario B) and says whether credit and funding markets agree. Equity stress alone usually fades; stress that spreads into credit and funding is what turns a correction into a crisis.
  2. Look at the four scenario scores and which way they moved. The dark tile is the highest. The change since the last update and over five days matters more than the level: a score climbing 15 points in a week is the warning.
  3. Scan "Changed since the last update". These are the indicators that just crossed a line. This list is the reason to open the dashboard at all on a quiet day.
  4. Check the AI funding chain. If contagion is spreading link by link and the credit pillar turns red, the AI selloff is becoming a balance-sheet problem rather than a valuation problem.
  5. Only then dig into a tab. Global, US, AI and Taiwan list every indicator with its one-year line; Backtest shows what happened after similar setups.

The lights

NormalWatchDangerInfoNo data

Every indicator has a watch line and a danger line, shown as dashed amber and red lines on its chart. Fixed lines are anchored to past episodes (for example, the high-yield spread peaked near 5.3% in late 2018 and near 11% in March 2020). Indicators marked as percentile-based are judged against their own history, using only the data that existed at each point in time.

A single red light is not a signal. Look for several lights in the same layer turning together, and for lights in a slower layer (credit, funding) following a fast one (equities).

Layer stress

Each layer averages the stress of its indicators on a 0–100 scale: 0 is calm, 50 sits right on the watch line, 100 means everything is past danger. Equities, volatility and Asia move first and fastest. Credit and funding move slowly and rarely, which is why they carry more weight when they do move.

The four scenarios

A. Deleveraging correction (like late 2018 or August 2024). Equities panic while credit and funding stay calm. Historically repaired within months. The score falls if credit or funding stress rises, because then it is no longer this pattern.

B. Earnings-downgrade bear (like 2000). AI stocks fall, policy is tight and growth slows. This is the slow, multi-year path. It gets a bump if a hyperscaler announces a capex cut.

C. Credit event (like mid-2007 or SVB in 2023). Stress spreads from equity into borrowers and lenders: high-yield and BBB spreads, private credit, regional banks, AI-linked debt.

D. Liquidity crisis (like September 2008 or March 2020). Everything is sold at once, even Treasuries; the dollar spikes; repo rates jump above the Fed's rate.

The scores measure how strongly today's data matches each pattern. They are not probabilities and do not add up to 100. The weights are judgment calls, not statistically fitted; the Backtest tab shows the score each past episode would have produced so you can check them.

Three switches that would change the view

  1. Hyperscalers cut capex. Watch the AI funding chain tab and the earnings checklist. This moves the story from valuation to earnings, toward scenario B.
  2. Credit confirms. High-yield spread above 4.5% or BBB above 2%, private credit underperforming, CoreWeave or Oracle CDS making new highs. This moves toward scenario C.
  3. Treasuries and stocks fall together, the dollar spikes, SOFR jumps above IORB. The "stocks/bonds/gold down together" and funding rows catch this. This is scenario D, and the one that forces central banks to act.

The AI funding chain

Risk is assumed to travel from the weakest balance sheets to the strongest: neoclouds such as CoreWeave, then Oracle, then Nvidia and memory makers, then the cash-rich hyperscalers. A link counts as cracked when its 20-day return falls below its trigger, and only if every link before it has cracked too. Spreading in order means contagion; the hyperscalers falling while upstream holds is a different story.

The composite light has three pillars: the credit alarm, monetization at the source (Anthropic and peers against the earnings checklist), and capex surprises. One failing is amber. Two or more failing together is red, because a single negative rarely breaks the chain on its own.

The news-based credit signals (CDS, bond yields, GPU loan rates, H100 rental prices) have no free data feed, so a weekly job reads the news and updates them every Sunday.

Taiwan

Margin data shows forced selling. When margin calls hit, investors sell what they can, so locked limit-down small caps push selling into TSMC and the ETFs. The deleveraging progress row divides the fall in margin loans by the fall in the index since its peak; above 1 means leverage is being cut faster than prices are falling, which has tended to come near the end of a selloff (the Backtest tab tests this once enough history is loaded). The maintenance ratio here is an estimate on a different basis from brokers' figures, so read its direction rather than its level.

Backtest and analogs

The comparison table lines up every past selloff at the same number of trading days after its peak as today, so you are comparing like with like. The conditional table asks: the last times this exact condition was true, what did the market do over the following months? The analog table finds the days since 2000 whose overall picture (drawdown, VIX, credit, rates, dollar, gold, stock-bond correlation) looked most like today.

Samples are small: there have only been about a dozen major selloffs since 1998. Use these to calibrate expectations, not to forecast.
